First time? Set up your Picoone-off · takes a minute

The Pico needs the programmer firmware loaded onto it once. After that it stays on the Pico — you won't need this again.

Pico 1 ↓ The original Raspberry Pi Pico, Pico H, Pico W Pico 2 ↓ Pico 2 and Pico 2 W — it says "Pico 2" on the board
  1. Download the file for your Pico above.
  2. Hold down the small BOOTSEL button on the Pico while plugging it into USB, then let go. A drive called RPI-RP2 appears, like a USB stick.
  3. Copy the .uf2 file onto that drive.
  4. The drive disappears on its own — that's the Pico restarting with the programmer loaded. You're done.
Picked the wrong file? No harm done — the Pico simply ignores firmware built for the other model. Just copy the right one across.

Wiring

MAX V CPLD — Pico pins 4-7: GP2 TCK · GP3 TMS · GP4 TDI · GP5 TDO, ground on pin 3.

ATmega32A — Pico pins 9-12: GP6 SCK · GP7 MOSI · GP8 MISO · GP9 RESET, ground on pin 8.

Always connect ground between the programmer and your board — a shared power supply isn't enough. Both boards must run at 3.3 V; the Pico's pins are not 5 V tolerant.

Which CPLD do I have?

The 5M40ZE64, 5M80ZE64 and 5M160ZE64 all report the same JTAG ID (0x020A50DD) — MAX V doesn't encode density in its ID, and all three share a package. Reading the ID confirms the board is powered and wired correctly, but can't tell you which of the three you're holding. Your SVF names its target device, so this page reads that and shows it before programming. Check it matches the marking on your chip.

Making the files

CPLD — in Quartus, once per build:
quartus_cpf -c -q 12.0MHz -g 3.3 -n p design.pof design.svf

AVR — use the .hex your compiler or IDE produces. In Microchip Studio it's under Debug\ or Release\; with avr-gcc and a Makefile it's whatever your avr-objcopy step writes. Drop the .hex, not the .elf.

Programming erases the chip first, so whatever was on the board before is replaced. That's normal — keep your source, not the board, as the copy that matters.

Checking a board without changing it

Tick Verify only before dropping a .hex and the programmer compares every byte of the chip against the file without erasing anything. Use it to confirm which build a board is running, or to check a board you don't want to disturb.

Fuses

Your board ships set up correctly, so for normal coursework you can ignore this. If you need to change the clock speed or free up PORTC, the fuse panel appears once an AVR is detected.

The presets cover the settings you're likely to want — clock speed, and freeing PORTC 2-5 by switching off the chip's on-board debug interface. All of them keep ISP enabled, so none can lock you out.

If a board does stop responding after a fuse change, it's usually recoverable. A wrong clock setting means the chip has no clock — feed a slow square wave into its XTAL1 pin and it programs again. The one setting that genuinely can't be undone from here is the serial programming fuse (SPIEN); this programmer refuses to write it, but other tools will let you, and recovery afterwards needs specialist equipment. Get in touch rather than buying a replacement.

Changed the clock? Update F_CPU in your code to match, or your delays and serial baud rate will be wrong.
